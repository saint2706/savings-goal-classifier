\begin{abstract}
A savings-product or financial-inclusion team cannot inspect tens of thousands of households by hand, so outreach triage has to be automated. We study this task on the India Human Development Survey-II (41,518 households, 2011--12), using a normative label of retaining at least 20\% of annual income after consumption. Because the label is an accounting identity, expense-to-income ratios reproduce it at 99.75\% agreement. Composition shares look safer yet leak jointly: food spending tracks household size, so an unfitted formula from household size, food share and income ranks households at ROC-AUC 0.895. A simulation ties this leak to one regression residual, and HCES 2022--23 reproduces the same expenditure link. Our headline classifier therefore uses onboarding covariates only (income, demographics and debt), scoring ROC-AUC 0.882 under PSU-grouped cross-validation and 0.886 on held-out units. For the prespecified primary objective of fixed-budget need screening at a 25\% budget, it reaches 36.2\% at-risk recall against a 36.7\% ceiling (98.5\% precision; weighted recall 35.4\%), 1.1 points above poorest-first (95\% CI 1.0--1.3). Dropping caste and religion leaves 25\%-budget recall unchanged (0.362) while degrading subgroup calibration on smaller groups. Rankings hold under income under-reporting stress tests and dissaver trimming. Under hypothetical nudge assumptions, risk ranking moves fewer households than random contact, while reachability moves more by shifting outreach toward middle-income urban households. Within IHDS-II, rankings hold across held-out states and rural--urban shifts while calibration drifts until a 10\%-PSU intercept shift corrects it; out-of-time classifier accuracy remains untested because HCES omits income.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Savings products in India target a population where most households record little or no surplus. Outreach carries a per-contact cost under a fixed budget, and a programme team cannot evaluate tens of thousands of households manually. The operational task is triage: label a household as on track or at risk and allocate the next contact accordingly.

We study this triage as binary classification on the India Human Development Survey-II (IHDS-II), covering 42,152 Indian households in 2011--12 \cite{ihds2}. Unlike NSS consumption surveys, IHDS-II records income and category-level expenditure for the same household; dropping 634 rows with non-positive income or missing consumption leaves 41,518 households in 2,461 primary sampling units (PSUs).

Two constraints govern modelling and evaluation. First, the label is arithmetic: a household is on track when it retains at least 20\% of annual income after consumption. Expense-to-income ratios reproduce it at 99.75\% agreement. Composition shares pass univariate checks yet leak jointly: given household size and income, food share approximately recovers total expenditure and the label. Second, the outreach objective must be fixed before picking a ranking rule. Because IHDS-II records savings status with no intervention arm, we prespecify \emph{fixed-budget need screening} (at-risk recall and contact precision at a 25\% budget) as the primary objective, and test hypothetical threshold-crossing nudges and rupee-shortfall capture separately as sensitivity checks.

\subsection{Scope and objectives}

We pursue five objectives: (1) establish empirically and via residual simulation ($\sigma(\log\text{food}\mid\log\text{size})$) when spending shares leak; (2) benchmark six model families against two baselines under PSU-grouped validation for the prespecified need-screening objective, separating recall, precision and ceiling capture in sample and survey-weighted estimates; (3) audit grouped SHAP attributions, subgroup errors and calibration, and the effect of dropping sensitive attributes (\texttt{Caste\_Group}, \texttt{Religion}); (4) test sensitivity to income measurement error, external non-accounting holdings and hypothetical cost-and-response nudge scenarios without causal uplift claims; and (5) assess cross-survey leakage stability (HCES 2022--23), within-IHDS-II state/sector transfer, 10\%-PSU recalibration and nominal income scaling.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

Predicting household welfare from survey covariates underpins proxy means testing when income is unobserved. Brown, Ravallion and van de Walle found high exclusion errors among the poorest across nine African countries despite strong regression fit \cite{brown2018}, paralleling our finding that strong AUC does not ensure large targeting gains over simple rules. McBride and Nichols showed that out-of-sample validation and machine learning improve poverty targeting \cite{mcbride2018}, and Blumenstock et al. predicted poverty from mobile metadata \cite{blumenstock2015}. Our task predicts a savings residual rather than consumption level, making spending features prone to joint leakage.

Following Kleinberg et al. \cite{kleinberg2015}, we treat triage as a prediction policy problem: the survey identifies status rankings, whereas causal outreach response requires randomised campaign data. Reminders raise saving \cite{karlan2016} while access constraints bind poor households \cite{dupas2013}, motivating our hypothetical response scenarios.

Indian surveys show systematic divergence between income and consumption \cite{deaton2005} (here 55.9\% spend above income), and Engel's law appears at Spearman $\rho = -0.280$. Leakage audits \cite{kaufman2012} usually target single columns rather than joint reconstruction. We use gradient-boosted trees \cite{chen2016} with exact tree SHAP \cite{lundberg2017,lundberg2020}, treat budget shares on Aitchison's simplex \cite{aitchison1982} with isometric log-ratio coordinates \cite{egozcue2003} and multiplicative zero replacement \cite{martinfernandez2003}, and select $k$ by silhouette \cite{rousseeuw1987}, Davies--Bouldin and Calinski--Harabasz indices.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Data, target and prespecified objectives}

The analysis dataset is built from ICPSR study 36151, DS0002 (Household). IHDS-II records 52 consumption items across 30-day and annual recall windows, annualised as $12 \times \text{monthly} + \text{annual}$ and mapped to 11 expense categories from the codebook. Their sum matches the survey aggregate \texttt{COTOTAL} at correlation 0.9932 (median difference zero, 97.7\% within 1\%, label agreement 99.75\%). Dominant occupation breaks ties (18\% of households) toward the steadier income stream. Missing debt (\texttt{DB5}, 7.6\%) is preserved with a missingness indicator rather than imputed to zero.

The binary target is
\begin{equation}
    \texttt{Goal\_Met} = \mathbf{1}\left[\frac{\texttt{INCOME} - \texttt{COTOTAL}}{\texttt{INCOME}} \geq 0.20\right],
    \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
in annual 2011--12 rupees. Exactly 31.93\% of sampled households are on track (68.07\% at risk, a 2.13:1 imbalance), or 30.47\% on track (69.53\% at risk) with survey weights \texttt{WT}. Because 20\% is a normative convention rather than a self-reported goal, we report threshold sensitivity: the at-risk share shifts from 68.1\% at 20\% to 61.7\% at 10\% and 74.7\% at 30\% (about 93.5\% label agreement). Models therefore measure normative savings capacity, not personal goal alignment.

We prespecify \emph{fixed-budget need screening} as the primary operational objective: rank households by predicted risk $1 - \hat{P}(\texttt{Goal\_Met}=1)$ and evaluate at-risk recall, precision and ceiling capture at a 25\% budget (sample and survey-weighted). Two secondary objectives are tested separately to expose objective dependence: (i) hypothetical threshold-crossing nudge targeting, ranked by a reachability-band estimator $\hat{P}(s \ge 0.20-\delta) - \hat{P}(s \ge 0.20)$, and (ii) rupee-shortfall targeting via quantile regression on \texttt{Savings\_Rate}.

\subsection{Multivariate leakage audit}

Raw rupee categories and expense-to-income ratios reconstruct~\eqref{eq:target} at 99.75\% agreement and are excluded. Composition shares sum to one and pass single-feature checks (largest marginal $| \text{AUC} - 0.5 |$ is 0.100 for healthcare share). Jointly, however, food spending scales with household size (log elasticity 0.630, $R^2 = 0.292$), so $\texttt{COTOTAL} \approx k \cdot \text{size} / \text{food share}$. With $k$ equal to median annual food spend per person (\rs 8,848), the unfitted score $1 - (k \cdot \text{size} / \text{food share}) / \texttt{INCOME}$ ranks households at ROC-AUC 0.895 (excluding only 24 zero-food-share rows, 0.06\%). Under grouped CV ablation, dropping food share alone costs 0.002 AUC (remaining shares substitute), dropping the size family costs 0.010, and dropping all shares and participation flags retains 94.4\% of full-model AUC (0.879 vs 0.931). By our pre-set decision rule (exclude shares if the formula exceeds 0.85 AUC or the no-share model keeps most of full AUC), spending shares are retained for diagnosis only. Six survey-reported savings-instrument indicators sit outside consumption arithmetic and are held out for external validation (fixed-deposit holding is 15.2\% among on-track households vs 8.2\% among at-risk).

\subsection{Features, sensitive attributes and validation}

We compare three feature sets. The \emph{deployable} headline set (13 features) contains log income, household size, age dependents (0--14 or 60+), dependency ratio, head age, highest adult education, debt-to-income, a debt indicator, a missing-debt indicator and four one-hot categoricals (occupation, area type, caste group, religion) with explicit \texttt{Unknown} levels. Because caste and religion are sensitive attributes, we also evaluate an 11-feature \emph{no-sensitive} deployable set that drops \texttt{Caste\_Group} and \texttt{Religion}. The \emph{full} diagnostic set (29 features) adds the 11 shares and five participation indicators for majority-zero categories.

One PSU-grouped fold of \texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold} (8,299 households in 472 PSUs) was held out before any design choice. All selection and tuning use five-fold PSU-grouped CV on the remaining 33,219 households because neighbours share prices, labour markets and interviewers. Debt-to-income is winsorised at the 99th percentile inside each fold. Linear models apply standard scaling and omit food share as reference; trees are unscaled. \texttt{RobustScaler} was rejected because zero-inflated shares have near-zero interquartile ranges, inflating entertainment shares above 150. Feature checks confirm these choices: expense-to-income ratios transfer worst across income halves on a neutral target (\texttt{Has\_Bank\_Savings}, mean AUC 0.603 vs 0.628 for rupees and 0.618 for shares), participation flags raise full-set AUC from 0.917 to 0.919, centred log-ratios lose (0.911 vs 0.920), and missing debt is informative (goal attainment 0.367 vs 0.315). Models are compared on macro-F1, ROC-AUC, at-risk PR-AUC (prevalence 0.681), Brier score, log-loss and 10-bin expected calibration error (ECE).

\emph{Measurement-error sensitivity and external holdings:} Trimming the 22.0\% extreme dissavers ($\texttt{COTOTAL} > 2 \times \texttt{INCOME}$, $n=32{,}381$, risk 59.1\%) keeps AUC 0.843 and doubles the model's 25\%-budget recall edge over poorest-first from $+1.1$ to $+2.5$ points (95\% CI 2.1--2.8; $+2.6$ when refit on that subset, AUC 0.842, ECE 0.006). Under multiplicative income under-reporting stress tests ($+10\%$ to $+30\%$ uniform, or $+25\%$ farm/business, $+5\%$ salaried, $+15\%$ other), the original risk ranking retains ROC-AUC 0.873--0.881 and widens its 25\%-budget edge to $+1.7$--$+2.8$ points ($+2.0$ heterogeneous), while at-risk prevalence falls from 68.1\% to 53.7\%. Extreme under-reporting compresses the model's ranking edge while invalidating absolute prevalence claims. Against non-accounting formal holdings, raw income beats the savings-adequacy score (ROC-AUC 0.724 vs 0.679 for fixed deposits, 0.701 vs 0.647 for pension/LIC, 0.630 vs 0.574 for bank savings), because larger households face lower per-capita savings rates while having more adults able to hold formal accounts. Peer-benchmark checks likewise remain inconclusive: at-risk households out-spend peers in rupees in every category (mechanical) and devote higher shares to healthcare ($+4.4$ pp) and education ($+2.8$) but less to food ($-9.0$), while ``closable'' shortfall swings from 92.8\% (rupee peers) to 22.3\% (share peers). On the secondary rupee-gap objective, quantile shortfall regression reaches 45.5\% of total gap at 25\% vs 37.2\% (classifier) and 28.7\% (poorest-first), though heavily weighted by extreme dissavers.

\subsection{Hypothetical outreach under assumed response}

Because IHDS-II records no campaign or intervention, all outreach cost and conversion figures below are explicitly \emph{hypothetical sensitivity scenarios}, not estimates of causal uplift. We assume a contacted household responds with probability $r$, responders raise their savings rate by $\delta = 5$ pp of income, a household crosses the benchmark only if within $\delta$ below 0.20 (4.6\% of at-risk households), and each contact costs \rs 150. We test three assumed response profiles: uniform $r=0.20$, income-gradient $0.05\text{--}0.35$ (access constraints \cite{dupas2013}), and debt-burdened $0.30/0.10$, alongside $\delta \in \{2.5, 10\}$ pp and \texttt{Debt\_Missing} assignments.

At a 25\% budget under uniform $r=0.20$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:business}(c)), hypothetical households moved per 1,000 contacts are 0.8 for risk ranking (0.5 weighted), 2.2 for poorest-first, 6.2 for random contact, 10.1 for the reachability-band estimator (9.9 weighted) and 25.0 for an oracle. Hypothetical cost per moved household is \rs 199,615 (risk; \rs 285,454 weighted), \rs 24,028 (random) and \rs 14,885 (reachability; \rs 15,225 weighted); break-even requires a moved household to be worth more than that figure and scales linearly with contact cost. The hypothetical reachability edge over risk is 9.3 per 1,000 (95\% PSU-bootstrap CI 8.4--10.1), 11.3 (10.3--12.2) under income-gradient response, and 10.1 (8.9--11.0) under debt-burdened response (9.9 and 9.6 when \texttt{Debt\_Missing} receives 0.20 or 0.10), holding at $\delta = 2.5$ and 10 pp, with survey weights, and without sensitive attributes (weighted reachability 9.8, 11.3 and 10.7 across the three scenarios). Switching from need screening to reachability also changes \emph{who} is contacted: risk ranking selects 88.3\% of income decile 0 and 33.2\% of less-developed villages (11.7\% of metro urban), whereas reachability selects 2.0\% of decile 0, 42.0--42.7\% of deciles 5--7 and 32.4\% of metro urban (\texttt{results/outreach.json}). Validating actual response requires randomised campaign data (for which \texttt{savings\_goal.models.uplift} provides a T-learner).

\subsection{External validity, within-survey transfer and nominal scaling}

No public Indian survey after 2011--12 records both income and consumption for the same household, so out-of-time classifier accuracy cannot be tested. Four checks establish what can be verified. \emph{Leakage mechanism (HCES 2022--23):} On 261,746 HCES households \cite{hces} (no income, so the label is not rebuilt), food-on-size elasticity is 0.565 vs 0.630 in IHDS-II, residual sd is 0.472 vs 0.485, and weighted food share is 0.465 vs 0.466; our simulation places both surveys at implied oracle AUC 0.868 (empirical IHDS-II 0.895). The leakage warning therefore persists into 2022--23, while classifier performance out of time does not follow from this check. \emph{Nominal scaling breakdown:} Nominal per-capita spending rose 2.36$\times$ (rural) and 2.51$\times$ (urban) between IHDS-II and HCES. Feeding $2.4\times$ unadjusted nominal income into the 2011--12 model on held-out PSUs inflates predicted on-track prevalence from 32.2\% to 62.9\% against 31.9\% observed (CITL $+31.0$ pp, ECE 0.310), proving 2011--12 income thresholds and models require CPI deflating and local recalibration. \emph{Within-IHDS-II transfer:} Holding out whole states in five folds gives ROC-AUC 0.871--0.890 (beats poorest-first by $+0.7$ to $+1.6$ recall points in every fold); rural-to-urban AUC is 0.866 and urban-to-rural is 0.882. \emph{Recalibration:} Calibration drifts across these tested shifts (state-fold CITL $-7.1$ to $+3.9$ pp; rural-to-urban $+9.1$ pp; urban-to-rural $-4.8$ pp). A 1-parameter logit intercept shift fitted on 10\% of target PSUs (evaluated on the remaining 90\% disjoint PSUs) reduces state-fold CITL to $[-0.8, +1.4]$ pp (worst ECE $0.069 \to 0.022$) and sector drift to $-0.4$ pp rural-to-urban (ECE $0.091 \to 0.018$) and $-2.3$ pp urban-to-rural ($0.051 \to 0.030$) at fixed AUC.

\section{Discussion}

Including spending shares raises ROC-AUC to 0.93, yet an unfitted three-column formula reaches 0.895 and removing shares costs only 0.047. Using onboarding covariates alone reaches held-out AUC 0.886 and improves fixed-budget need screening over poorest-first by 1.1 recall points at 25\% (narrow, consistent with \cite{brown2018}), rising to $+2.5$ points once extreme dissavers are excluded.

Four practical guidelines follow. First, check multivariate compositional leakage via $\sigma(\log\text{food}\mid\log\text{size})$ ($\leq 0.5$ lets shares plus size recover total spend); admit spending shares only when measured strictly before the prediction window, as enforced by our scoring service. Second, prespecify the outreach objective and report recall, precision and ceiling capture against simple rules; switching from need screening to threshold-crossing nudges shifts outreach from the poorest rural households to middle-income urban households. Third, drop sensitive attributes (\texttt{Caste\_Group}, \texttt{Religion}) from ranking when they add zero budget recall, but pair exclusion with subgroup calibration monitoring so minority groups (such as Sikh or Scheduled Tribe households) are not silently miscalibrated. Fourth, scope transfer claims to tested shifts: within-IHDS-II rankings hold across states and sectors while calibration fails without local intercept recalibration, and unadjusted nominal rupees break probabilities immediately under inflation.

Key deployment limits remain: income under-reporting (55.9\% spend above income; 22.0\% above twice income, invalidating absolute prevalence claims \cite{deaton2005}), 2011--12 survey vintage with untested out-of-time classifier accuracy, household-only grain, stock-only debt without repayment flows, normative 20\% convention, and absence of real campaign data (leaving all nudge conversion and cost figures strictly hypothetical).

\section{Conclusion}

On 41,518 Indian households, spending shares leak the savings-adequacy label jointly through household size. An onboarding classifier without shares scores held-out ROC-AUC 0.886 and adds 1.1 recall points over poorest-first for prespecified need screening (+2.5 points excluding extreme dissavers). Dropping caste and religion preserves 25\%-budget recall yet creates subgroup calibration drift requiring monitoring. Under hypothetical nudge assumptions, risk ranking trails random contact while reachability shifts outreach toward middle-income urban households; real campaign data is required to estimate uplift. Within IHDS-II, rankings hold across states and sectors after 10\%-PSU recalibration, while modern deployment needs CPI re-basing and out-of-time validation.
